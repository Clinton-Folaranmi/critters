Interactive animal studies · method

Creature Study Field Manual

A method for building interactive, top-down animal scenes that hold up under close inspection. It covers research, body construction, motion, habitat, lighting, performance and QA, with example figures from finished work. It applies to any animal; four reference animals (a frog, a koi, a robin and a ground beetle) serve as examples throughout. Send it with the brief template at the end.

This is the illustrated edition of docs/CREATURE_STUDY_SPEC.md, the single source of truth, written for language models and people alike. Rule IDs in brackets, such as (P-3) or (E-12), point to its numbered MUST and SHOULD rules; the rules index, the time-of-day targets, the locomotion summary and the QA table here are generated from it. The frog pond in this repository is the worked example.

01 / Principles

Ten rules

A scene reads as fake because of a small set of errors: a foot sliding while the body turns, a swimmer rotating in place, a pose changing in a single frame, a shadow cast toward the light. These rules target those errors, and the sections after them explain how to meet each one.

P-1 · Research the animal firstWatch slow-motion footage before designing any pose. A frog swimming in a straight line kicks both hind legs at once; most people guess they alternate.
P-2 · Give every action a motiveA robin turns toward a sound, a beetle steers around a twig, a koi rises for food. Nothing moves to fill time or because the page loaded.
P-3 · Pin feet that touch a surfaceThe frog's feet stay on the lily pad while its body turns over them. Feet only move by stepping.
P-4 · In water or air, turn only while movingA koi changes heading along a curve as it swims. It can't rotate on the spot.
P-5 · Ease every pose changePose values move toward their targets over several frames. A landing squash still takes 20 to 30 ms.
P-6 · Keep mirrored motion mirroredPaired limbs move together until steering, loading or a stumble gives them a reason to split.
P-7 · Use one key lightEvery shadow falls away from it and every highlight faces it.
P-8 · Build depth from physical cuesA visible floor, a medium that hides it with depth, and shadows offset by height.
P-9 · Keep idle behaviour smallBreathing, blinking and looking around. Larger actions come at irregular intervals for each animal.
P-10 · Measure, then review by eyeFoot slide, pose jumps, brightness and frame time all get numbers before anyone signs off.

03 / Process

Order of work

Research and a written motion sheet come before code. The study ships only after the measurements pass.

A failed measurement gets fixed in the simulation. Adjusting the rendering to hide it doesn't count as a fix.

Research means slow-motion footage and any locomotion papers you can find, summarised in your own words. Copy nothing. For the robin, that might be how often it hops versus runs across a lawn, and how far it crouches before take-off. The motion sheet turns those notes into a plan: body dimensions in body lengths, a table of key poses with timings for each behaviour, how individuals differ, the layout of the scene, what a viewer should see in 45 seconds of watching, and what each tap does. Someone with authority over the result agrees the sheet before the build starts.

04 / Structure

Simulation and rendering are separate

The simulation decides what happens and the renderers draw it. Keeping them apart lets two renderers show identical scenes, makes tap detection exact, and allows frame-by-frame testing.

The simulation never draws. It reports events (ripples, taps), and the controller and renderers respond to them.

Measure the world in fractions of the canvas height with y pointing up, and measure each animal in its own body lengths with the head toward +x, so everything scales with the screen. Seed all randomness so a session can be replayed exactly. Clamp the time step to about 50 ms so a stalled tab can't throw anything across the scene. Keep the controller free of any framework; the same code then runs inside an app and as a single offline HTML file. Put the colours and copy for each lighting version in one theme module that both renderers import. If the GPU loses its context, switch to the 2D renderer immediately and retry the GPU a couple of times later. Frame stepping, forced fallback and other developer tools belong in development builds only, and debug information should only appear on screen when requested with a flag.

05 / Anatomy

Body construction

Bodies are made of a few overlapping ellipses blended together. Limbs are short chains of tapered capsules rooted at a hip or shoulder, with toes fanning from the end. The frog below is the reference build; the koi replaces limbs with a spine, and the robin adds wing chains.

Dashed outlines are the individual shapes and the tint is their blend. Orange lines are the bones the pose system moves.

These are distance shapes, so the GPU renderer shades them per pixel, the 2D renderer traces the same outlines, and taps are tested against exactly what's drawn: touching the frog's toe or the koi's tail fin counts as touching the animal. Author every dimension in body lengths, and give each individual its own size, colouring, markings and rhythm, including breathing rate and voice. The body has three values of its own. Stretch squashes it along the spine while keeping its area, lean shifts the weight forward or back, and slip twists the body slightly away from its heading. Keep joints visibly bent from the camera's angle, since a straight chain reads as a stick figure; full extension belongs to the moment of pushing. Markings and highlights should sit in the body's own coordinates so they move with it. Small signs of life, like a frog's throat pulse, a robin's head tilt or a beetle's twitching antennae, add more than any large action.

06 / Pose

Pose values and easing

Each limb has one value from folded (0) to extended (1) that bends every joint in the chain together, plus a yaw value that swings the whole limb around its root. The robin's wing uses the same idea with a fold value. Behaviours set targets, and the values ease toward them.

Dashed lines show what to avoid. Orange lines show the fix.

Never set a pose value more than one frame's worth of motion away from where it is. Ease toward targets with an exponential approach, v += (target − v) · (1 − e^(−rate · dt)), or follow an authored curve. The change can be quick as long as it spans a few frames; a landing squash still takes 20 to 30 ms to arrive. Build each behaviour as curves between the key poses in the motion sheet (load, push, gather, reach, contact, settle). Only stagger paired limbs when the real animal does, for example the lead leg of a turning jump pushing about 50 ms ahead of the other, or a beetle's legs moving in two alternating sets of three.

07 / Behaviour

Behaviour states and motives

Each animal is in exactly one named state at a time, and each state has its own pose curves. The loop below is generic. For the frog, Load and Act are the crouch and the leap. For the robin they're the crouch before take-off and the flight. For the beetle, Act is a short burst of running.

Orient only runs when the animal faces well away from a destination it has already chosen.

Every action needs a motive that exists in the world: food, a threat, a need like rest, or a destination. Nothing happens because the page loaded, and nothing is staged for the viewer. Stagger the first actions (for example at 4.5, 7 and 10 seconds) and give each individual its own jittered timers so they never move in sync. In a calm scene an animal might do something larger every 10 to 20 seconds and rest the rest of the time. Keep turning on the spot under about 45 degrees and finish larger turns during the crouch and the movement itself. An animal that has committed, such as a frog in mid-air, can flinch at a tap but can't change course, and a short cooldown after each tap prevents resets and teleports. Calm animals should prefer destinations roughly ahead of them. Steering away from edges and from each other should start early enough that hard boundaries almost never act.

Give the world a check() function that lists every broken rule it can detect: two animals claiming one perch, a reservation nobody holds, a non-finite position, an animal outside the visible area. The list should stay empty through every test run.

08 / Contact

Contact and foot planting

A foot touching a surface is pinned to a point on that surface, and the leg re-aims at the pin every frame. The frog does this on the lily pad, the robin on its branch, and the beetle with every foot that isn't mid-step.

The pinned point never moves and the leg adapts to it. In a turn of about 40 degrees the body's swing eases in and out while the feet step one or two at a time; the frog's short front legs step twice.

Store each pin in the surface's own coordinates, so a floating lily pad or a swaying branch carries the foot with it. Each frame, before drawing, swing the leg around its root until it points at the pin and solve how far it has to extend to reach it. The body can then breathe, lean and pivot while the foot stays put.

A step has four stages. The foot releases its pin, and at that moment you work out where it should rest relative to the body when this step ends. The pin then moves there along an eased path that curves slightly toward the body, which is how a lifted foot looks from above, and the toes close on the way. Finally the foot lands, the toes spread, and it's pinned again.

A settled animal keeps every touching foot pinned, so breathing moves the body over still feet. Turning on a surface happens by stepping, never by rotating the whole rig; pivot near the hindquarters so neither end outruns the reach of its legs. Plan steps around reach: a leg whose root is being carried away from its foot steps during the swing, before it straightens, and short legs take two small steps instead of dragging. While the frog crouches to jump, its back feet keep their grip, its front feet ease off as the weight moves back, and every foot lets go over the first frames of the push. An animal on a moving support, like the robin on a swaying branch, inherits the support's movement and rotation in every state, including landing and crouching. Cap how far a planted leg can stretch; beyond a compact reach, let the foot slip slightly rather than pulling the leg straight (C-1 to C-7).

Measure it. npm run qa:motion reports how far each planted foot sits from its pin and how far it slides per planted stretch; the target is 0.06 body lengths. The frog meets it at the median but not in the tail: a forefoot pinned beyond its compact reach moves a little with each breath, a known issue the spec records (its section 15) until the pins are re-set by a settling step.

09 / Locomotion

Locomotion catalogue

Every way of moving gets the same four things written down before it is built: its pose keys, what is pinned and when (contact), how it turns, and how it is measured (L-1 to L-3). On the ground, feet are pinned and heading changes by stepping. In water or air there's nothing to push against sideways, so heading changes only while the body travels, along a curve. The koi shows this most clearly; the frog follows the same rule when it swims, and so does the robin when it banks in flight.

Change heading by curvature multiplied by the distance actually travelled, measured after any collision or boundary has clamped the position. The exception is a stroke pushing against a wall or bank, which should count its push, or an animal facing the edge can never turn back. Let the stroke set the curvature: straight strokes allow a gentle drift, and a steering stroke allows a tighter turn. For the koi, the rest of the spine then follows the path the head took.

Keep mirrored strokes mirrored. A frog swimming straight draws both knees in, kicks both legs back together, and holds a long, still glide. Unevenness only appears when steering: the outside leg drives fully and the inside one kicks a little later and shorter. Spread mid-air turns across the whole flight with an ease in and out, as the robin does when it banks between branches. Ease transitions between media too: a frog entering water dips slightly deeper before rising to swim, and leaving it clears the surface over a few frames. Drive trailing limbs from a smoothed turn rate (leg yaw of roughly minus the rate times a gain, capped near 0.4 radians) and let the body lag its path slightly. A startled escape reads best as a very short gather followed by a hard steering stroke that curves away from the threat.

Walking gaits

A gait is a footfall timeline. Stance feet are pinned; speed comes from the stride and the cycle rate, never from sliding stance feet (L-5).

On the ground every stance foot is pinned (C-1), and the body moves over it. Each swing carries its foot to where it will rest relative to the body at the end of its next stance, on an eased arc curving slightly toward the body. Turning, the body yaws no faster than its stance feet can follow, and the outside legs take longer strides (L-6, L-7). The frog's turn on a lily pad is the worked example of a turn on the spot: the forefeet take two short steps each, inside first, and each hind foot steps once, the outside one mid-swing.

Hopping and jumping

Six keys, from the frog's motion sheet: sit; a loading crouch (190 ms calm, 75 ms startled, 160 ms from the water) with the weight back over the hips; the launch, the lead hind leg driving back first and the other 52 ms later; the apex, legs relaxing, body barely larger; the landing compression, forefeet reaching before contact and the body squashing to 0.81; and the recovery, one small after-bounce into the sit. Every foot is pinned until the push and lets go over its first frames; the forefeet are pinned on touchdown (L-8). A turn of more than about 43° finishes in the crouch and is spread across the flight (L-9). The shadow keeps its ground-sized footprint: height only offsets, softens and fades it (L-10).

Limb-driven swimming

Recovery (both knees draw in together, about 340 ms), kick (120 ms, mirrored, webs open, all the speed), glide (1.15–1.85 s, visibly still). Only a steering stroke is uneven (L-11). Nothing is pinned. Heading changes by curvature times distance (L-12), and trailing legs swing to the outside of the turn (L-13).

Swim homing. A frog swims for 3 to 7 seconds. Over the last two it comes round toward the nearest free lily pad, then keeps to that one while it stays free rather than dithering between two. A pad in the way is swum round on the side the frog is already heading for, and it climbs onto its pad, or any free one it meets. Measured over two hours untouched: a median swim of 6.6 s and a longest of 25 s on a wide screen (L-15).

Leg clearance. Keeping the body's centre in the water isn't enough: turning back from the shore, the trailing hind legs slipped under the bank. After posing, every knee, ankle and toe is tested against the shore with its drawn thickness, and the frog is moved straight off the bank by the largest shortfall. Swimmers' bodies and legs over the bank now measure zero through every soak (L-16).

Spines, wings, perches, slithering and burrowing

A spine-driven swimmer (koi, tadpole, eel) takes its heading from the head's path and the rest of the spine follows it, with a wave running head to tail that grows toward the tail; nothing slides sideways (L-17 to L-19). A flyer banks along a curve, tucks its feet after take-off and reaches with them to land, and holds still in a glide (L-20 to L-23). A percher keeps both feet pinned in the perch's frame so its sway carries the bird, and turns by a hop in place or a shuffle (L-24, L-25). A slitherer's body follows the head's path, sliding sideways only when sidewinding, when its two short contact segments are pinned (L-26, L-27). A burrower braces against the tunnel while its forelimbs dig, advances only into space it has dug, and shows depth continuously with spoil where it dug (L-28, L-29).

10 / Habitat

Habitat layers

The habitat is drawn in physical layers from the ground up. The frog and koi's pond, the lawn under the robin's branch and the beetle's leaf litter all use the same stack. Anything static is drawn once per canvas size.

Read from the bottom. On dry land the medium and surface layers become air: haze and dappled light.

The ground needs to be rich and irregular, with no visible grid or repeating tile. Scatter objects on jittered cells, mix a few large ones with many small ones, sink some partway into the ground with ragged edges, and add darker organic patches, fine grain, and tint from the environment: algae on the pond stones, moss on the beetle's log, dry grass under the robin. Small debris should collect where it would in reality, such as pebbles along a bank or leaves against a log.

Anything floating or raised casts two shadows: a tight contact shadow and a softer shadow on the ground below, offset further the deeper or higher it is. A lily pad over deep water has its ground shadow well away from it; a robin on a low branch has one close by. That offset is the strongest depth cue available. Every effect needs a physical source: a glow needs a light, and a sparkle needs a surface and a sun. Repeating light patterns such as caustics are cheapest drawn once as a tile that repeats without a visible seam, with two copies sliding past each other at different scales and warped slightly by the surface. Both renderers should use the same tile.

11 / Environment design

Environment design

The habitat isn't a backdrop. It sets where animals can go, what covers them, what a tap hits, how the picture meets the page, and what gives the animals reasons to move. Every rule here was a real bug or lesson in the frog study.

A tap goes to the topmost thing drawn at that point, and nothing outside the picture's outline takes one.

Boundaries match what's drawn

One function defines where the animals may go (the frog's shoreDistance), shared by the world, both renderers and the hit test, with the shader's copy generated from it and checked to float precision (E-1). Containment keeps every drawn part off the boundary, not only the centre, and scenery the animals use is kept clear of it too: a drifting lily pad is nudged back off the bank (E-2, E-3). Details that mark the boundary, like pebbles at the waterline, are placed along the shore the world uses (E-4).

Occluders, draw order and taps on them

Write down, per state, what is drawn over what. A frog on a pad or in the air is drawn over the bank, so a leap near the shore passes over the grass; a swimmer is drawn under it (E-6). A tap goes to the topmost thing drawn there: grass leaning out over the water and the reeds count as bank, unless a frog drawn over them is there (E-7). Every tap on scenery shows something: a tap on the grass lifts a few fireflies, or midges by day, and ripples the water if it's close to the edge (E-8). Occluders cast shadows like everything else (E-9).

The picture's edge

The scene has a designed edge where it meets the page: for the frog, a squarish oval made ragged and soft. Nothing outside it takes a tap and the cursor turns back to an arrow there; the keyboard focus ring follows the same outline, a little outside it, as an ink line on a pale halo (E-11 to E-14).

Composition across aspect ratios

The scene works from a 375-pixel phone to a wide screen without horizontal scroll. Positions that matter get a layout per shape, blended by aspect: the frog's pads have a wide and a narrow layout, so on a phone the stages don't crowd, and on a resize they drift to their new homes. Static layers are re-painted only after the size holds, and scenery placed along the shore with its own seeds barely changes when the canvas grows a pixel (E-15 to E-19).

Affordances as motives

The habitat offers what behaviour needs, and behaviour uses it: perches to rest on, water to cross, cover, food. The frog pond has four stages for three frogs, so a swimmer always has somewhere to go, and only pads big enough and near enough count; the rest are scenery the frogs leave alone (E-20 to E-22).

Ambient life

Ambient life has a source and a place: fireflies gather in three clusters and leave the lower pond quiet, and motes rise only where the grass was tapped. It is a small fixed pool, drawn only inside a box around each speck, and the animals notice it (E-23 to E-25).

Wind and weather

Wind is one field owned by the world and read by everything that moves in it; gusts travel, so a meadow shows a wave passing rather than everything bending at once. Weather is a palette plus particles, each with its own brightness target, and animals respond to it with motives (E-26 to E-29). The frog pond has neither yet; its reeds only sway with time.

Time of day

A time of day is a palette on the same scene, geometry and behaviour, with its own copy and a brightness target measured inside the visible picture (E-30 to E-32). The frog's Night and Day are the example; the targets are in section 12.

12 / Light and colour

Lighting, colour and depth

Each lighting version (night, day, overcast, moonlight) is a palette applied to the same scene: the light's colour and direction, the medium's colours and cloudiness, how much sky it reflects, ground materials, the strength of moving light, plant colours, shadow strength and vignette.

One light direction governs every shadow, and each shadow's offset shows how high or deep something is. The depth field also controls how much floor shows and where debris collects.

Check shadow direction deliberately, because it's an easy mistake to miss: contact shadows, body shadows and ground shadows all fall away from the light, while sheens, rim light and eye highlights face it. Keep every colour in the palette module and none in the renderers. The 2D renderer should be within about 5 brightness points of the GPU renderer in every palette (E-31; npm run qa:luma measures mean luma inside the visible picture, bank included). In low light, let the floor show mostly near the light source, and let raised details like stones or a beetle's wing cases catch warm light on the side facing it. Finish with a light vignette set by the palette, fine grain, and a designed edge where the scene meets the page.

13 / Performance

Performance

A frame at 60 Hz lasts 16.7 ms, and the scene should finish well within that on a modest laptop GPU. It must also be a good neighbour on an ordinary page: small to download, no stalls while it loads, and nothing running when it can't be seen.

The frog demo: 4.9 KB of script with the page, 66 KB (26 KB gzipped) as the pond comes near; a WebP still of the first frame shows until the live one is ready. Measured by qa:size and qa:first.

Load the scene's code only as its container comes within about 300 pixels of the viewport, and show a still of its first frame until then; the world waits until the first live frame, so the two match (F-1, F-2). Compile shaders in the background with KHR_parallel_shader_compile and paint static layers in a worker with OffscreenCanvas, keeping finished layers for reuse, with a fallback to painting on the page (F-3, F-4). Run the loop only while the scene is on screen and the tab visible (F-5). Cap the pixel ratio at 2 and step the resolution down when frames keep running slow, never back up (F-6). Strip comments and whitespace from shaders at build time, and load the Canvas fallback only when WebGL fails (F-7, F-8).

Per-pixel loop discipline (F-9). A full-screen loop costs its iterations on every pixel. Adding six rare motes to the frog's two per-pixel firefly loops (8 to 14 iterations) cost three milliseconds a frame; giving the motes their own loop inside a box around them, with each speck skipping its exponentials beyond its reach, won it back. Keep full-screen loops short and box rare effects.

Frog pond at 1520 × 1028 on an entry-level laptop GPU. Drawing the ground once and using a caustic tile recovered more than half the frame.

Keep the page visible while measuring, because hidden tabs throttle or skip GPU work. Force the GPU to finish after each draw (reading back one pixel is enough), average about 40 frames, and subtract the fixed overhead measured with the same loop on a tiny canvas. Redraw static layers only when the canvas size changes. Limit each animal's detailed shading to a radius around it, and check that radius against the animal's largest real extent over a long test run: the frog's legs at full push or the robin's open wings should stay under about nine-tenths of it. Keep per-frame garbage out of the hot path (F-11), and release every GPU resource, observer and listener on teardown (F-12). To find what's expensive, return early after each layer in turn and time it, then start with the largest.

14 / Interaction

Interaction and accessibility

Every tap should produce a visible response. Tapping the frog makes it leap away from the tap; tapping the water near the koi sends it curving off; tapping the robin's branch makes it flinch or fly; tapping near the beetle makes it change course. Taps are tested against the drawn shapes, so a toe, a fin or a wingtip counts. Touch input gets a slightly larger hit area than a mouse pointer. The scene is reachable by keyboard with a visible focus ring, and Enter or Space triggers a central action, such as dropping a pebble into the pond.

With reduced motion turned on, run no animation loop. Draw one finished still, describe it for screen readers as a still image, and hide the tap instructions. Give the live scene a label that points to its instructions, and write the title, caption and labels separately for each lighting version. The layout must work at 375 pixels wide by switching to a taller aspect ratio, without horizontal scrolling. A single self-contained HTML version that works offline, with no external requests, is also worth shipping: it needs a doctype, a language, a charset, a viewport and no body margin, and it should start its own script as its worker so it works as one file, even in a sandboxed frame (I-1 to I-8).

Your first animal: a beetle in leaf litter

20 / Rules index

Rules index

Every numbered rule in the spec, generated from it. MUST rules are requirements; SHOULD rules need a written reason to skip. Section numbers inside a rule refer to the spec.

1. Principles

P-1MUST Research the animal before designing any pose: slow-motion footage and locomotion papers, summarised in your own words (copy nothing). A straight-swimming frog kicks both hind legs at once; most people guess they alternate.
P-2MUST Give every action a motive that exists in the world (food, a threat, rest, a destination). Nothing moves to fill time or because the page loaded.
P-3MUST Pin feet that touch a surface. A pinned foot moves only by stepping.
P-4MUST In water or air, change heading only while moving, along a curve.
P-5MUST Ease every pose change over several frames; nothing snaps in one frame except inside a deliberate push or kick.
P-6MUST Keep mirrored motion mirrored. Paired limbs split only for steering, loading or a stumble.
P-7MUST Use one key light per variant. Every shadow falls away from it; every highlight faces it.
P-8SHOULD Build depth from physical cues: a visible floor, a medium that hides it with depth, shadows offset by height.
P-9SHOULD Keep idle behaviour small (breathing, blinking, looking) and larger actions irregular and individual.
P-10MUST Measure, then review by eye. Every check in section 12 is run and reported before anyone signs off.

2. Architecture

A-1MUST The world has no DOM, canvas or timer access. It exposes step(dt), input methods (tap, a keyboard action), resize(aspect), geometry for renderers and hit tests, and check(). It runs unchanged in Node.
A-2MUST World units: fractions of the canvas height, y up, x from 0 to the aspect ratio. Animal units: body lengths, head toward +x. Everything then scales with the screen.
A-3MUST Seed all randomness in the world, so a session can be replayed exactly (the QA soaks depend on it).
A-4MUST Clamp the time step (the frog uses 50 ms) so a stalled tab can't throw anything across the scene.
A-5MUST The scene controller is framework-free (plain DOM), so the same code runs in an app, in a plain page and in a single offline HTML file. It is mounted with one call that returns a handle with dispose().
A-6MUST Colours, looks and all visible copy for every variant live in one theme module that every renderer reads. The GPU shader is generated from it; no renderer holds its own colour literals.
A-7MUST Renderers draw the same world. Shared geometry (shore distance, depth, reed blades, the picture's outline) is written once in TypeScript, and where a shader needs it the GLSL is generated from, or checked against, that code (qa:parity).
A-8MUST A GPU renderer and a Canvas 2D fallback. If the GPU context is lost or a shader fails, switch to the fallback at once and retry the GPU a couple of times later (the frog: after 2.5 s, twice). The fallback's code loads only when it's needed.
A-9MUST The world reports events (ripples, taps and what they hit) instead of drawing them; the controller and renderers respond.
A-10MUST Dev-only tools (frame stepping, forced fallback, fixed resolution, console hooks) are behind a build-time flag (import.meta.env.DEV) and absent from shipped builds (qa:smoke, qa:standalone). Debug information appears on screen only when asked for with a flag.
A-11MUST check() lists every broken rule the world can detect (two animals claiming one perch, a reservation nobody holds, a non-finite pose, an animal outside where it may be). It stays empty through every soak.
A-12SHOULD The package exports the scene (mountX), the theme and its types from the main entry, and the world and the shore maths as separate DOM-free entries, so tools and tests can import them in Node.

3. File layout

A-13MUST One file per concern, named for the animal or habitat. The world and habitat files import nothing from the DOM.
A-14SHOULD A study adds its qa:* checks by extending qa/thresholds.mjs and qa/run.mjs rather than writing separate harnesses.

4. Body and pose

B-1MUST Bodies are a few overlapping ellipses blended together; limbs are short chains of tapered capsules rooted at a hip or shoulder, with toes fanning from the end; spines are chains of 8–13 points with a width profile. All dimensions in body lengths.
B-2MUST Draw and hit-test the same shapes. Renderers draw the capsules the world computes; taps are tested against them (animalDistance), so a toe, a fin or a wingtip counts (qa:hit, qa:tol).
B-3MUST Each limb is driven by one extension value (0 folded … 1 extended) that bends every joint together, plus a yaw about its root, plus a spread for toes or feathers. Behaviours set targets; pose() turns values into capsules every frame.
B-4MUST Ease values toward targets with v += (target − v) · (1 − e^(−rate·dt)), or follow an authored curve between key poses. A change may be quick but spans several frames (a landing squash: 20–30 ms). Largest one-frame changes are measured by qa:motion.
B-5MUST The body has three values of its own: stretch (squash along the spine, area kept), lean (weight forward or back) and slip (a small yaw off the heading).
B-6SHOULD Keep joints visibly bent from the camera; a straight chain reads as a stick figure. Full extension belongs to the push.
B-7MUST Individuals differ: size, colouring, markings, breathing rate, rhythm. Markings and highlights live in body coordinates so they move with it.
B-8SHOULD Small signs of life (a throat pulse, a head tilt, antennae) add more than any large action.

5. Behaviour

H-1MUST Each animal is in exactly one named state; each state authors its own pose curves. The frog: sit, turn, crouch, air, land, swim, dive.
H-2MUST Every action has a motive in the world (P-2). Stagger the first actions and give each individual its own jittered timers so they never move in sync.
H-3MUST A committed animal (in the air, mid-strike) may flinch at a tap but can't change course. A cooldown after each tap (the frog: 0.35 s) prevents resets and teleports.
H-4MUST Turns on the spot stay small (the frog: at most ~43°); a larger change of heading finishes in the crouch and during the movement itself.
H-5SHOULD Calm animals prefer destinations roughly ahead. Steering away from edges and each other starts early enough that hard boundaries almost never act.
H-6MUST No temporary state lasts forever: a swim, a flight or a search has an end and a way home (qa:soak fails a state that lasts more than 40 s).
H-7SHOULD Write down what a viewer should see in 45 seconds of watching, and measure it (the frog: qa:swims for swim lengths and hop rate).

6. Contact and planting

C-1MUST A foot touching a surface is pinned to a point stored in the surface's frame, so a floating pad or a swaying branch carries it. Every frame, before drawing, the leg is re-aimed at its pin (yaw) and its reach solved (extension).
C-2MUST A settled animal keeps every touching foot pinned; breathing moves the body over still feet.
C-3MUST A foot moves by stepping: release the pin, work out where it should rest relative to the body when the step ends, carry the pin there on an eased path that curves slightly toward the body (toes closing), set it down (toes spreading), pin again.
C-4MUST Turning on a surface happens by stepping, never by rotating the whole rig. Pivot near the hindquarters so neither end outruns its legs' reach; short legs take two small steps rather than dragging.
C-5MUST Plan steps around reach: a leg whose root is carried away from its foot steps before it straightens. Cap a planted leg's stretch; beyond a compact reach the foot may give slightly rather than the leg being hauled straight — and that give is measured (qa:motion).
C-6MUST At take-off every foot lets go over the first frames of the push, as the body is driven away; at landing the forefeet reach before contact and are pinned on touchdown.
C-7MUST An animal on a moving support inherits the support's movement and rotation in every state, including landing and crouching.

7. Locomotion catalogue

L-1MUST Pick the locomotion modes from research (P-1) and write their pose keys and timings into the motion sheet before building them.
L-2MUST Every mode states its contact rule: what is pinned, when a pin is released, where it is set down.
L-3MUST Every mode states its turning rule, and turning is measured (turning while still, curvature, angular acceleration).
L-4MUST Transitions between modes are eased and physical (a frog entering water dips deeper, then rises; leaving, it clears the surface over a few frames).
L-5MUST Stance feet are pinned to the ground (C-1). Speed comes from the stride and the cycle rate, never from sliding stance feet.
L-6MUST Turning on the ground: the body yaws at most as fast as the stance feet can follow; the swing target for each foot is where it will rest relative to the body at the end of its stance. A turn on the spot is a sequence of short steps pivoting near the hips (the frog's on-pad turn is the worked example: forefeet two short steps each, inside first; each hind foot once, the outside one mid-swing).
L-7SHOULD Legs on the outside of a turn take longer strides; the body leans into the turn slightly.
L-8MUST Pins: all feet pinned through the sit and the crouch (hind feet grip in the crouch; forefeet ease off as the weight goes back); every pin released over the first frames of the push (C-6); forefeet pinned at touchdown.
L-9MUST Turning: on the ground only by stepping (L-6), up to ~43°; the rest of a larger turn happens in the crouch (≤ 2.2 rad/s) and is spread across the flight with an ease in and out. A committed jump doesn't change course (H-3).
L-10MUST The shadow keeps the ground-sized footprint; height only offsets it along the light, blurs it and fades it.
L-11MUST Straight strokes are mirrored exactly. A steering stroke (wanted heading more than ~15° off) is the only asymmetric one: the outside leg recovers further and drives fully, the inside leg kicks up to 40 ms later and ~30 % shorter.
L-12MUST Heading changes by curvature × distance actually travelled, measured after containment has clamped the position (curvature 3–20 per unit distance for the frog). A stroke pushing against a bank counts its push, or an animal facing the edge could never turn back.
L-13MUST Trailing limbs swing to the outside of a turn from a smoothed turn rate (yaw ≈ −rate × gain, capped near 0.4 rad), and the body lags its path slightly, so a turn reads as a bend.
L-14MUST Nothing is pinned in water. A startled escape is a very short gather and a hard steering stroke curving away from the threat, deeper under the surface.
L-15MUST A swim has an end and a way home: over its last seconds the animal comes round toward its exit and keeps to one target (swim homing, below).
L-16MUST Every part of a swimmer that is drawn stays on the water, not only its centre (leg clearance, below).
L-17MUST Heading comes from the head's path; the rest of the spine follows the path the head took (each point trails the one ahead at a fixed spacing). Nothing slides sideways.
L-18MUST The wave runs head to tail; its frequency rises with speed; its amplitude grows toward the tail.
L-19MUST Fins are flexible flaps driven by the local turn rate and speed; pectoral fins trail in turns and scull when hovering.
L-20MUST Nothing is pinned in the air; feet tuck after take-off and reach before landing, and are pinned on touchdown (C-6).
L-21MUST Heading changes by banking along a curve (P-4); the body rolls into the turn (seen from above: the near wing foreshortens), and the tail fans to steer.
L-22MUST Flap timing comes from research (a robin ~ 10–15 beats/s in climbing flight; glides between bursts). A glide holds still: no flapping in a glide.
L-23SHOULD Hovering (hummingbirds, dragonflies) is a figure-of-eight stroke with the body held; yaw in a hover only with a reason (tracking prey).
L-24MUST Both feet are pinned in the perch's frame (C-1), so the perch's sway carries the bird and its tail balances against it.
L-25MUST Turning on a perch is a hop in place or a shuffle of short steps; the body never spins over still feet by more than the legs allow.
L-26MUST In undulation, concertina and rectilinear, the body follows the head's path: no sideways slip. Contact segments (concertina, sidewinding) are pinned to the ground while in contact.
L-27MUST Heading changes only as the head advances; the rest follows.
L-28MUST The bracing parts are pinned to the tunnel wall while the forelimbs dig; the body only advances into space that has been dug.
L-29MUST Depth is shown continuously (the animal sinks under a soil layer that thickens with depth; a raised ridge or spoil appears where digging happened), never by a jump cut.

8. Environment design

E-1MUST One function defines the boundary (the frog: shoreDistance(x, y, aspect), positive on water, negative on the bank), shared by the world, both renderers (GLSL generated from the same numbers) and the hit test. JS and GLSL agree to float precision (qa:parity: ≤ 1e-5, no sign flips).
E-2MUST Containment keeps each animal's body off the boundary with a clearance (the frog: 0.012 plus a margin of 0.8 BL inside the open-water oval), and checks every drawn part, not only the centre (7.3, leg clearance).
E-3MUST Scenery the animals use is kept clear of the boundary too: a drifting pad is nudged back off the bank (clearance 0.015; qa:soak: pad-under-bank frames 0; closest rim measured 0.011).
E-4MUST Anything the world treats as the boundary is visibly the boundary: the painted bank's waterline follows shoreDistance, and details (pebbles at the waterline) are placed along the visible shore, at their intended distance from the water.
E-5SHOULD Where the scene continues out of the frame (the frog's water runs off the top and right), the swimmable area ends a little inside the picture's edge so animals never swim into the fade.
E-6MUST Decide per state what is drawn over what, and write it down. An animal in the air or on a raised perch is drawn over ground scenery (a frog's leap near the shore passes over the grass); a swimmer is drawn under the bank.
E-7MUST A tap goes to the topmost thing drawn at that point. Scenery drawn over the animals' layer (grass overhanging the water, reeds) takes the tap as scenery (the frog: bank-layer coverage ≥ 0.35 from a ¼-size mask the painter returns, or reedAt()), unless an animal drawn over that scenery is there (a frog on a pad or in the air still wins). qa:hit: ≥ 85 % of opaque grass over the water counts as bank (measured 92–93 %).
E-8MUST Every tap on scenery produces a visible response (the frog: a tap on the bank or reeds lifts 4 motes — fireflies at night, midges by day — that drift off over ~1.6 s, and ripples the water if within 0.05 of the waterline).
E-9MUST Occluders cast shadows in the key light's direction like everything else (the frog's reeds, both renderers).
E-10SHOULD An occluder's geometry is one data layout used by every renderer and the tap test (the frog: reedBlades(), uploaded to the shader as uniforms).
E-11MUST The scene has a designed edge where it meets the page (the frog: a squarish oval, a superellipse with exponent 2/0.6, radii (aspect/2 − 0.05, 0.45), made ragged and soft with turbulence, applied as a CSS mask).
E-12MUST No interaction outside the visible picture: taps where the edge is faded out do nothing and the cursor turns back to the default (pictureEdge(x, y, aspect) ≤ 1 accepts; qa:hit: 0 taps accepted where the mask is under 0.2; ≤ 0.5 % of the clearly visible picture outside the rule, the ragged fringe).
E-13MUST The keyboard focus ring follows the same outline, a little outside it (0.03 world units), as an ink line on a halo so it reads over dark water and the page alike (the scene sets --pond-ring and --pond-ring-halo mask images on the container's parent).
E-14SHOULD Until the scene applies its edge, the container shows a plain soft oval, so nothing appears with a hard rectangle.
E-15MUST The layout works from a phone (375 px wide, aspect 0.91) to a wide screen (aspect 1.48) with no horizontal scroll (qa:a11y).
E-16MUST Positions that matter (perches, stages) have a layout per shape, blended by aspect, not the same shares of the width stretched (the frog: PAD_HOMES_WIDE at 1.48 and PAD_HOMES_NARROW at 0.91, blended with smoothstep; stages rim to rim ≥ 0.035 apart at 0.91). On resize, things drift to their new homes; before the first frame or in the still they snap there.
E-17MUST Static layers are re-painted for a new size only after the size has held (a true debounce: the frog waits 200 ms; qa:bake: ≤ 1 bake asked for during a drag), and the old layer is shown stretched meanwhile.
E-18SHOULD Scenery placement is stable under small resizes: items placed along the shore with per-item seeds (qa:reshuffle: a 1 px wider canvas changes ≤ 2 % of bank pixels, measured 0.8 %; 10 px ≤ 15 %, measured 9.4 %).
E-19SHOULD There is a still of the first frame for each shape and variant (section 10).
E-20MUST The habitat offers the things behaviour needs, and behaviour uses them: perches or stages to rest on, water or open ground to move through, cover to hide in, food to catch. The frog: four stages (three occupied, one spare, so a swimmer always has somewhere to go — qa:soak reports frames with no free stage, 0 measured), open water for swims, fireflies to track and catch.
E-21MUST Only usable things count as affordances: a stage must be big enough for the animal and within reach of the swimmable water (the frog: pads beyond 0.8 pond radii are scenery, and frogs leave them alone).
E-22SHOULD Scenery that isn't an affordance is visibly different (the frog's small bud pad, pads drifting off the edges).
E-23MUST Ambient life has a source and a place: the frog's fireflies gather in three clusters (near the left stage, right of centre, mid-pond) and leave the lower pond quiet; midges by day; motes only where the grass was tapped.
E-24MUST It is sparse and cheap: a fixed pool (the frog: 8 flies, 6 motes), each drawn only inside a box around it (section 10, the motes regression).
E-25SHOULD Animals notice it (eyes follow fireflies; a tongue strike when one drifts within reach, then 6–14 s of rest).
E-26MUST Wind is one field (direction, strength, gusts over time) owned by the world and read by everything that moves in it: grass, reeds, leaves, the water's ripples, an animal's feathers or balance. Nothing sways on its own clock.
E-27MUST Gusts travel: a gust reaches things upwind first, so a meadow shows a wave passing, not everything bending at once.
E-28MUST Weather is a palette plus particles (section 9): rain adds drops and ring ripples on water and darkens the ground; overcast removes hard shadows and caustics; each weather variant has its own brightness target.
E-29SHOULD Animals respond to weather with motives (shelter under a leaf in rain, feathers fluffed in cold), not decoration.
E-30MUST A time of day is a palette applied to the same scene, geometry and behaviour (the frog's Night and Day differ only in FROG_PALETTES, copy, and fireflies becoming midges). Copy, title and labels are written for each variant.
E-31MUST Each variant has a brightness target: mean luma (0–255, Rec. 601 weights) inside the visible picture, bank included, where the picture's mask is over half opaque. The renderers stay within 5 of each other (qa:luma).
E-32MUST The key light's direction may change between variants but each variant has one; shadows follow it (P-7). qa:midge checks a small flier's shadow in both renderers.

9. Light, palettes and colour

V-1MUST Each variant's palette holds the light's colour and direction, the medium's colours and cloudiness, sky reflection, ground materials, moving light strength, plant and prop colours, ambient life colours, ripple colours, shadow strength and vignette. No colour literals in renderers (A-6).
V-2MUST Build depth physically: a depth field (the frog: shelfDepth(), shallow at the bank, deepest mid-pond, the same in both renderers), a medium that hides the floor more with depth, ground shadows of floating or raised things offset further and softer the deeper or higher they are.
V-3MUST Every effect has a physical source: a glow needs a light, a sparkle a surface and a sun, caustics a sun and clear water.
V-4MUST Static ground is painted once per size (the frog's bed and bank) and never moves; repeating light patterns (caustics) are a seamless tile drawn once, two copies sliding past each other at different scales, faded over deep water, the same tile in both renderers.
V-5MUST Check shadow direction deliberately: contact, body and ground shadows fall away from the light; sheens, rim light and eye highlights face it.
V-6SHOULD In low light the floor shows mostly near the light, and raised details catch warm light on the side facing it.
V-7MUST Finish with a light vignette set by the palette, fine grain, and the designed edge (E-11).

10. Performance

F-10MUST Limit each animal's detailed shading to a radius around it, and check the radius against the animal's largest real extent over a long run (legs at full push under ~0.9 of it).
F-11MUST No per-frame garbage in the hot path: scratch arrays hoisted, uniforms written by index (qa:alloc: world.step ≤ 1 KB per frame, measured ~0.1–0.5 KB).
F-12MUST Release everything on dispose: the loop, observers, listeners, the canvas and its GPU resources (qa:lifecycle: 80 switches, heap flat, 0 canvases and 0 animation frames after dispose).
F-13SHOULD First load has no main-thread task over 50 ms (qa:first, limit 80 ms for noise; measured none on a quiet M1, Phase 1). Frame cost ≲ 15 ms GPU at 1520 × 1027 (qa:gpu; measured 10.3 night, 11.4–13.0 day on the M1 in Phase 1). Timing checks depend on the machine: compare against a baseline built in the same session (qa/tools/baseline.mjs).

11. Interaction and accessibility

I-1MUST Every tap produces a visible response: the animal reacts (the frog leaps away from the tap; a swimmer dives and curves away), a prop reacts (a pad dimples, bobs and drifts with a small contact ripple), the medium reacts (a full ripple), scenery reacts (E-8).
I-2MUST Taps are tested against drawn shapes (B-2); touch gets a larger target than a mouse (the frog: 0.022 vs 0.01 world units; qa:tol).
I-3MUST The scene is focusable (tabindex="0", role="button"), labelled per variant, and points to its instructions (aria-describedby). Enter or Space triggers a central action (the frog: a pebble in the middle), prevents scrolling and ignores key repeat (qa:hit).
I-4MUST A visible focus ring that follows the picture's edge (E-13).
I-5MUST With reduced motion: no loop at all; one finished still; role="img" with the still's own description; not focusable; the instructions hidden (qa:rm).
I-6MUST A variant switch (the frog: Night | Day) is real buttons with aria-pressed (or links with aria-current when each variant has its own address); the variant may also be chosen in the address (#day).
I-7MUST Text contrast ≥ 4.5:1 and non-text (the switch's outline) ≥ 3:1 against the page in every variant (qa:a11y).
I-8MUST A single self-contained HTML file that works offline: a doctype, lang, UTF-8, a viewport meta, body { margin: 0 }, no external requests, the worker started from the page's own script, working in a sandboxed iframe without storage (qa:standalone; the build fails if any basic is missing).

12. QA

Q-R1MUST Run npm run check (typecheck, lint, format, docs, build, Node checks) before every commit, and npm run qa:browser before a release or after any visual, input or loading change.
Q-R2MUST A failed measurement is fixed in the simulation or the renderer that causes it, not hidden by adjusting another layer.
Q-R3MUST Report what was verified and what wasn't (machines, browsers, real devices, how the timing was taken and the load average).
Q-R4MUST Review enlarged frame-by-frame crops of each key moment (a turn, a take-off, a landing, a stroke, a steering turn) in both renderers (qa:shots, qa:stills).

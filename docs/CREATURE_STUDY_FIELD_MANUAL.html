Interactive animal studies · method

Creature Study Field Manual

A method for building interactive, top-down animal scenes that hold up under close inspection. It covers research, body construction, motion, habitat, lighting, performance and QA, with example figures from finished work. It applies to any animal; four reference animals (a frog, a koi, a robin and a ground beetle) serve as examples throughout. Send it with the brief template at the end.

01 / Principles

Ten rules

A scene reads as fake because of a small set of errors: a foot sliding while the body turns, a swimmer rotating in place, a pose changing in a single frame, a shadow cast toward the light. These rules target those errors, and the sections after them explain how to meet each one.

1. Research the animal firstWatch slow-motion footage before designing any pose. A frog swimming in a straight line kicks both hind legs at once; most people guess they alternate.
2. Give every action a motiveA robin turns toward a sound, a beetle steers around a twig, a koi rises for food. Nothing moves to fill time or because the page loaded.
3. Pin feet that touch a surfaceThe frog's feet stay on the lily pad while its body turns over them. Feet only move by stepping.
4. In water or air, turn only while movingA koi changes heading along a curve as it swims. It can't rotate on the spot.
5. Ease every pose changePose values move toward their targets over several frames. A landing squash still takes 20 to 30 ms.
6. Keep mirrored motion mirroredPaired limbs move together until steering, loading or a stumble gives them a reason to split.
7. Use one key lightEvery shadow falls away from it and every highlight faces it.
8. Build depth from physical cuesA visible floor, a medium that hides it with depth, and shadows offset by height.
9. Keep idle behaviour smallBreathing, blinking and looking around. Larger actions come at irregular intervals for each animal.
10. Measure, then review by eyeFoot slide, pose jumps, brightness and frame time all get numbers before anyone signs off.

03 / Process

Order of work

Research and a written motion sheet come before code. The study ships only after the measurements pass.

A failed measurement gets fixed in the simulation. Adjusting the rendering to hide it doesn't count as a fix.

Research means slow-motion footage and any locomotion papers you can find, summarised in your own words. Copy nothing. For the robin, that might be how often it hops versus runs across a lawn, and how far it crouches before take-off. The motion sheet turns those notes into a plan: body dimensions in body lengths, a table of key poses with timings for each behaviour, how individuals differ, the layout of the scene, what a viewer should see in 45 seconds of watching, and what each tap does. Someone with authority over the result agrees the sheet before the build starts.

04 / Structure

Simulation and rendering are separate

The simulation decides what happens and the renderers draw it. Keeping them apart lets two renderers show identical scenes, makes tap detection exact, and allows frame-by-frame testing.

The simulation never draws. It reports events (ripples, taps), and the controller and renderers respond to them.

Measure the world in fractions of the canvas height with y pointing up, and measure each animal in its own body lengths with the head toward +x, so everything scales with the screen. Seed all randomness so a session can be replayed exactly. Clamp the time step to about 50 ms so a stalled tab can't throw anything across the scene. Keep the controller free of any framework; the same code then runs inside an app and as a single offline HTML file. Put the colours and copy for each lighting version in one theme module that both renderers import. If the GPU loses its context, switch to the 2D renderer immediately and retry the GPU a couple of times later. Frame stepping, forced fallback and other developer tools belong in development builds only, and debug information should only appear on screen when requested with a flag.

05 / Anatomy

Body construction

Bodies are made of a few overlapping ellipses blended together. Limbs are short chains of tapered capsules rooted at a hip or shoulder, with toes fanning from the end. The frog below is the reference build; the koi replaces limbs with a spine, and the robin adds wing chains.

Dashed outlines are the individual shapes and the tint is their blend. Orange lines are the bones the pose system moves.

These are distance shapes, so the GPU renderer shades them per pixel, the 2D renderer traces the same outlines, and taps are tested against exactly what's drawn: touching the frog's toe or the koi's tail fin counts as touching the animal. Author every dimension in body lengths, and give each individual its own size, colouring, markings and rhythm, including breathing rate and voice. The body has three values of its own. Stretch squashes it along the spine while keeping its area, lean shifts the weight forward or back, and slip twists the body slightly away from its heading. Keep joints visibly bent from the camera's angle, since a straight chain reads as a stick figure; full extension belongs to the moment of pushing. Markings and highlights should sit in the body's own coordinates so they move with it. Small signs of life, like a frog's throat pulse, a robin's head tilt or a beetle's twitching antennae, add more than any large action.

06 / Pose

Pose values and easing

Each limb has one value from folded (0) to extended (1) that bends every joint in the chain together, plus a yaw value that swings the whole limb around its root. The robin's wing uses the same idea with a fold value. Behaviours set targets, and the values ease toward them.

Dashed lines show what to avoid. Orange lines show the fix.

Never set a pose value more than one frame's worth of motion away from where it is. Ease toward targets with an exponential approach, v += (target − v) · (1 − e^(−rate · dt)), or follow an authored curve. The change can be quick as long as it spans a few frames; a landing squash still takes 20 to 30 ms to arrive. Build each behaviour as curves between the key poses in the motion sheet (load, push, gather, reach, contact, settle). Only stagger paired limbs when the real animal does, for example the lead leg of a turning jump pushing about 50 ms ahead of the other, or a beetle's legs moving in two alternating sets of three.

07 / Behaviour

Behaviour states and motives

Each animal is in exactly one named state at a time, and each state has its own pose curves. The loop below is generic. For the frog, Load and Act are the crouch and the leap. For the robin they're the crouch before take-off and the flight. For the beetle, Act is a short burst of running.

Orient only runs when the animal faces well away from a destination it has already chosen.

Every action needs a motive that exists in the world: food, a threat, a need like rest, or a destination. Nothing happens because the page loaded, and nothing is staged for the viewer. Stagger the first actions (for example at 4.5, 7 and 10 seconds) and give each individual its own jittered timers so they never move in sync. In a calm scene an animal might do something larger every 10 to 20 seconds and rest the rest of the time. Keep turning on the spot under about 45 degrees and finish larger turns during the crouch and the movement itself. An animal that has committed, such as a frog in mid-air, can flinch at a tap but can't change course, and a short cooldown after each tap prevents resets and teleports. Calm animals should prefer destinations roughly ahead of them. Steering away from edges and from each other should start early enough that hard boundaries almost never act.

Give the world a check() function that lists every broken rule it can detect: two animals claiming one perch, a reservation nobody holds, a non-finite position, an animal outside the visible area. The list should stay empty through every test run.

08 / Contact

Contact and foot planting

A foot touching a surface is pinned to a point on that surface, and the leg re-aims at the pin every frame. The frog does this on the lily pad, the robin on its branch, and the beetle with every foot that isn't mid-step.

The pinned point never moves and the leg adapts to it. In a turn of about 40 degrees the body's swing eases in and out while the feet step one or two at a time; the frog's short front legs step twice.

Store each pin in the surface's own coordinates, so a floating lily pad or a swaying branch carries the foot with it. Each frame, before drawing, swing the leg around its root until it points at the pin and solve how far it has to extend to reach it. The body can then breathe, lean and pivot while the foot stays put.

A step has four stages. The foot releases its pin, and at that moment you work out where it should rest relative to the body when this step ends. The pin then moves there along an eased path that curves slightly toward the body, which is how a lifted foot looks from above, and the toes close on the way. Finally the foot lands, the toes spread, and it's pinned again.

A settled animal keeps every touching foot pinned, so breathing moves the body over still feet. Turning on a surface happens by stepping, never by rotating the whole rig; pivot near the hindquarters so neither end outruns the reach of its legs. Plan steps around reach: a leg whose root is being carried away from its foot steps during the swing, before it straightens, and short legs take two small steps instead of dragging. While the frog crouches to jump, its back feet keep their grip, its front feet ease off as the weight moves back, and every foot lets go over the first frames of the push. An animal on a moving support, like the robin on a swaying branch, inherits the support's movement and rotation in every state, including landing and crouching. Cap how far a planted leg can stretch; beyond a compact reach, let the foot slip slightly rather than pulling the leg straight.

09 / Water and air

Movement through water and air

In water or air there's nothing to push against sideways, so heading changes only while the body travels, along a curve. The koi shows this most clearly. The frog follows the same rule when it swims, and so does the robin when it banks in flight.

Left: the thing to avoid. Right: the heading stays along the path, and trailing limbs swing outward in proportion to the turn rate.

Change heading by curvature multiplied by the distance actually travelled, measured after any collision or boundary has clamped the position. The exception is a stroke pushing against a wall or bank, which should count its push, or an animal facing the edge can never turn back. Let the stroke set the curvature: straight strokes allow a gentle drift, and a steering stroke allows a tighter turn. For the koi, the rest of the spine then follows the path the head took.

Keep mirrored strokes mirrored. A frog swimming straight draws both knees in, kicks both legs back together, and holds a long, still glide. Unevenness only appears when steering: the outside leg drives fully and the inside one kicks a little later and shorter. Spread mid-air turns across the whole flight with an ease in and out, as the robin does when it banks between branches. Ease transitions between media too: a frog entering water dips slightly deeper before rising to swim, and leaving it clears the surface over a few frames. Drive trailing limbs from a smoothed turn rate (leg yaw of roughly minus the rate times a gain, capped near 0.4 radians) and let the body lag its path slightly. A startled escape reads best as a very short gather followed by a hard steering stroke that curves away from the threat.

10 / Habitat

Habitat layers

The habitat is drawn in physical layers from the ground up. The frog and koi's pond, the lawn under the robin's branch and the beetle's leaf litter all use the same stack. Anything static is drawn once per canvas size.

Read from the bottom. On dry land the medium and surface layers become air: haze and dappled light.

The ground needs to be rich and irregular, with no visible grid or repeating tile. Scatter objects on jittered cells, mix a few large ones with many small ones, sink some partway into the ground with ragged edges, and add darker organic patches, fine grain, and tint from the environment: algae on the pond stones, moss on the beetle's log, dry grass under the robin. Small debris should collect where it would in reality, such as pebbles along a bank or leaves against a log.

Anything floating or raised casts two shadows: a tight contact shadow and a softer shadow on the ground below, offset further the deeper or higher it is. A lily pad over deep water has its ground shadow well away from it; a robin on a low branch has one close by. That offset is the strongest depth cue available. Every effect needs a physical source: a glow needs a light, and a sparkle needs a surface and a sun. Repeating light patterns such as caustics are cheapest drawn once as a tile that repeats without a visible seam, with two copies sliding past each other at different scales and warped slightly by the surface. Both renderers should use the same tile.

11 / Light and colour

Lighting, colour and depth

Each lighting version (night, day, overcast, moonlight) is a palette applied to the same scene: the light's colour and direction, the medium's colours and cloudiness, how much sky it reflects, ground materials, the strength of moving light, plant colours, shadow strength and vignette.

One light direction governs every shadow, and each shadow's offset shows how high or deep something is. The depth field also controls how much floor shows and where debris collects.

Check shadow direction deliberately, because it's an easy mistake to miss: contact shadows, body shadows and ground shadows all fall away from the light, while sheens, rim light and eye highlights face it. Keep every colour in the palette module and none in the renderers. The 2D renderer should be within about 5 brightness points of the GPU renderer in every palette. In low light, let the floor show mostly near the light source, and let raised details like stones or a beetle's wing cases catch warm light on the side facing it. Finish with a light vignette set by the palette, fine grain, and a designed edge where the scene meets the page.

12 / Performance

Performance budget

A frame at 60 Hz lasts 16.7 ms, and the scene should finish well within that on a modest laptop GPU. The two largest savings are drawing static layers once and limiting detailed shading to the area around each animal.

Frog pond at 1520 × 1028 on an entry-level laptop GPU. Drawing the ground once and using a caustic tile recovered more than half the frame.

Keep the page visible while measuring, because hidden tabs throttle or skip GPU work. Force the GPU to finish after each draw (reading back one pixel is enough), average about 40 frames, and subtract the fixed overhead measured with the same loop on a tiny canvas. Redraw static layers only when the canvas size changes. Limit each animal's detailed shading to a radius around it, and check that radius against the animal's largest real extent over a long test run: the frog's legs at full push or the robin's open wings should stay under about nine-tenths of it. Cap the pixel ratio around 2, pause when the page is hidden, and release every GPU resource on teardown. To find what's expensive, return early after each layer in turn and time it, then start with the largest.

13 / Interaction

Interaction and accessibility

Every tap should produce a visible response. Tapping the frog makes it leap away from the tap; tapping the water near the koi sends it curving off; tapping the robin's branch makes it flinch or fly; tapping near the beetle makes it change course. Taps are tested against the drawn shapes, so a toe, a fin or a wingtip counts. Touch input gets a slightly larger hit area than a mouse pointer. The scene is reachable by keyboard with a visible focus ring, and Enter or Space triggers a central action, such as dropping a pebble into the pond.

With reduced motion turned on, run no animation loop. Draw one finished still, describe it for screen readers as a still image, and hide the tap instructions. Give the live scene a label that points to its instructions, and write the title, caption and labels separately for each lighting version. The layout must work at 375 pixels wide by switching to a taller aspect ratio, without horizontal scrolling. A single self-contained HTML version that works offline, with no external requests, is also worth shipping.

16 / Checklist

Definition of done

  • Research notes and a motion sheet are written and the direction is agreed.
  • The simulation is separate from the renderers, seeded, and clean through both long test runs.
  • Every value in section 14 is reported and passes.
  • Enlarged frame-by-frame crops of each key moment have been reviewed in both renderers.
  • Every palette meets its brightness target, and the 2D renderer is within about 5 points of the GPU renderer.
  • Frame time is about 15 ms or less, static layers are drawn once, and the shading radius has been checked.
  • Losing the GPU context falls back and recovers, and the console shows no errors.
  • Reduced motion, keyboard use, the focus ring and a 375-pixel layout all work.
  • The offline single-file version works.
  • A written change log lists what was checked and what wasn't.
